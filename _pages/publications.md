## Job Market Paper

### A Dynamic Model of Residential Sorting Using Aggregate Data: Welfare Implications of Urban Fracking
Presented in 2025 SEA Annual Meeting, and 2025 CU Environmental and Resource Economics Workshop.

[Current Draft (To Be Polished/Frequently Updated)](/files/JMP_Draft_Cho.pdf){: .btn .btn--primary}

**Abstract.**  
Urban fracking creates a local environmental disamenity, but its welfare consequences depend on how residents sort across neighborhoods, how rents and neighborhood composition adjust, and how residents value those changes over time. I study these forces in Tarrant County, Texas, using a dynamic residential sorting equilibrium model estimated from aggregate census data. Fracking restrictions reduce environmental exposure but generate sharply different welfare effects across demographic groups through equilibrium adjustments in rents and neighborhood composition and changes in expected future neighborhood values. A targeted ban in neighborhoods with high concentrations of poor Hispanic residents raises rents and induces poor Hispanic residents to move out, leaving the policy's intended beneficiaries worse off. Tightening well entry along the profitability margin benefits every demographic group in a myopic residential sorting model but makes some minority groups worse off when residents are forward-looking. Methodologically, I develop tractable procedures for estimating dynamic residential sorting and solving equilibrium counterfactuals using only aggregate location shares and moving probabilities, both commonly available in conventional census data.

## Working Papers

### Common Global Warming, Unequal Consequences in Local Income (with Yeonjik Lee)

**Abstract.**
We examine how the effects of global temperature shocks on income differ by group. Using disaggregated monthly income data and shocks in global temperature, we find robust evidence of rising global temperature widening the gap of income inequality. Following positive global temperature shocks, income losses are disproportionately concentrated among lower-income groups, while higher-income groups are less affected. These effects are considerably larger and more persistent for labor income than for capital income, indicating that climate shocks primarily operate through labor-market channels. Our findings highlight that income group structure is a key determinant of how climate shocks translate into distributional outcomes.
